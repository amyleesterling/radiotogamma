Deck 02 · the Observatory

One phenomenon. Sixteen orders.

The electromagnetic spectrum is one family of waves: electric and magnetic fields rippling through spacetime, carrying energy. Radio, microwaves, infrared, light, UV, x-rays and gamma rays are the same thing at different frequencies, from waves that pass through you unfelt to photons that snap molecules apart. The ribbon below is sixteen orders of magnitude of it, laid out as a ruler. Choose two signals, drag the probes, and read every number.

How matter commonly responds

The shelves above the ribbon are the mechanisms by which matter answers light at each frequency, lowest to highest: currents in antennas, spins flipping, molecules spinning, then vibrating, electrons jumping, bonds breaking, atoms ionizing, nuclei responding. The pictures are the wavelength scale ladder: each object sits where one wavelength is its own size.

These regimes overlap and depend on the material; they are common mechanisms, not walls. The named spectrum bands are conventions with soft edges, not laws of nature. Positions on the ribbon are exact; the icons are not to scale, since a city is fifty trillion atoms across.

Wave view

The length of each lane is the window you picked, so the number of cycles drawn is literally frequency times window. Only the animation speed is compressed: nothing can be shown moving at 1023 cycles a second. A wave too dense to resolve dissolves into a smear, which is the honest picture, because too fast to see is a blur rather than a strobe. Pick a shorter window and the fast lane slows into a wave while the slow lane flattens to a line.

Who senses what

The lens has zoomed to the stretch where every sense lives, 2 THz to 1.2 PHz, and the creature's range hangs over the ribbon. Different animals are issued different slices. Bees and birds see into the ultraviolet; pit vipers image the world in the infrared glow of their prey; your own skin senses radiant warmth far below what your eyes catch. And one correction to a common mix-up: no ear hears radio. Sound is pressure waves in air, not electromagnetic radiation: different physics that borrowed the same vocabulary. What gets through the air

The translucent surface is roughly what a clear sky at sea level lets down to the ground, high where the sky is open. Two doors are open: the radio window (the ionosphere turns back anything below about 10 MHz, and water vapour and oxygen close it again past about 30 GHz), and the optical window with a few infrared side doors between the water-vapour bands. Ozone shuts the door at 300 nm, and from there up the air itself absorbs everything: ultraviolet, x-rays and gamma rays are astronomy from orbit. Above about 100 GeV, past the right-hand end of this ribbon, gamma rays come back into reach from the ground, caught by the blue Cherenkov flash they make in the air. A dry mountaintop opens the millimetre and infrared cracks wider, which is why those telescopes live on Mauna Kea and the Chajnantor plateau, and why the infrared one that matters most went to space.

Redshift: space stretches the wave on the way

Redshift

The upper ribbon is the same spectrum redrawn where it arrives: every colour has slid toward the radio end by the same factor. Redshift is not a colour filter. Space itself has expanded while the light was in transit, and every wave in it stretched by the same factor, 1 + z, so a line emitted at one wavelength arrives at that wavelength times 1 + z, and its frequency and photon energy fall by the same factor. The lookback times come from the standard cosmology (flat, H0 = 67.7 km/s/Mpc, Ωm = 0.31), which puts the universe at 13.8 billion years old.

    Two signals, four quantities.

    Frequency fixes everything else about a wave in vacuum. Wavelength is the speed of light divided by it; period is one over it; the energy of each photon is Planck's constant times it. Whatever moves A to B moves all four in lockstep.

    Stage 04 · how far is 53 octaves

    One footstep to the Oort cloud.

    Everything on the ribbon, from the slowest radio rumble to the fiercest gamma ray, fits in a window of about 53 octaves of frequency. That is the entire observed electromagnetic range.

    An octave is one doubling. The word is borrowed from music: the A above middle C vibrates 440 times a second, and the next A up, at 880, is the same note one octave higher. Frequency works the same way everywhere, so the natural unit of the spectrum is not "plus so many hertz" but "doubled so many times". Radio to gamma is 53 doublings, stacked.

    Hear one doubling: Sound, not light: a pressure wave in air borrowing the spectrum's vocabulary. Nothing plays until you press.

    Doubling hides its own size. To feel it, take one walking step, 0.75 m, and double it once per octave. The named bands are marked at the octave where each begins:

    One step becomes seven-tenths of a light-year, deep in the comet halo at the solar system's edge, a sixth of the distance to the nearest star. Radio to gamma is the gap between a footstep and the Oort cloud, and the entire visible rainbow is a single stride somewhere along the way.
